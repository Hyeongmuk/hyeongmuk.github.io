How Does an Electron See Its Universe?

From Bloch electrons to topological and geometric band structures

October 2026·Approx. 25-minute read
INTRODUCTION

Why do materials behave so differently?

Condensed matter physics asks why materials made from the same elementary ingredients exhibit such radically different properties. Some conduct electricity almost effortlessly; others resist the passage of current. Some are magnetic, some superconducting, and others support electronic states with no simple counterpart in empty space. The aim is not merely to catalogue these differences, but to understand how they arise from the collective organization of electrons and atomic nuclei.

Electrical conduction gives a particularly sharp example. A familiar school-level explanation of resistance says that electrons cannot move freely because they collide with atomic nuclei. This picture captures the idea that something interrupts electronic motion, but it leaves a basic question unanswered. At room temperature, copper has an electrical resistivity of about 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m. Representative values for glass span roughly 10⁹–10¹⁴ Ω·m, and for hard rubber about 10¹³–10¹⁶ Ω·m. The contrast can therefore exceed twenty orders of magnitude, depending on the material and conditions.¹

The sizes and densities of atomic nuclei do not differ by anything remotely comparable. Why, then, can electrons move so easily through copper and so poorly through glass or rubber? And why can a small change in composition, temperature, or atomic arrangement completely alter a material's electrical character? The answer is not that electrons in one substance happen to encounter far fewer nuclei. The essential distinction is in their quantum states, in which states are available to carry current, and in how those states respond to perturbations.

To understand this, we must replace the image of an electron navigating a crowd of individual atoms with a description of the environment in which the electron's quantum wave propagates. In that sense, we can ask how an electron would describe the universe it inhabits. We begin with the simplest possible world: a perfectly periodic crystal.

The path from that question to present-day research did not unfold in a single leap. It passed through several concrete milestones: the discovery of Bloch states, the explanation of band filling, effective descriptions of imperfect crystals, and then the realization that eigenvectors possess both topological and geometric structure. Each milestone below is separated into a short statement box, with its original paper identified.

The presentation that motivated this essay posed four successive questions: what perfect periodicity means to an electron, what imperfections change, why equal dispersions need not imply equal states, and why states of the same topology can have different local geometry. The questions are useful as a narrative guide, not a substitute for a physical argument.

01

The universe of a perfect crystal

Periodicity changes what counts as an obstacle

A crystal is a solid whose atomic arrangement repeats under translations by lattice vectors. In the idealized picture, the potential experienced by an electron obeys V(r + R) = V(r) for every lattice translation R. Real materials are neither perfectly rigid nor perfectly ordered, but this idealization is a useful starting point: it isolates the consequences of periodicity before disorder, thermal motion, and interactions are introduced.

The one-electron Hamiltonian H₀ = p²/(2m) + V(r) contains the periodic lattice potential explicitly. In 1928, Felix Bloch established what is now called Bloch's theorem: its eigenstates can be chosen in a special form now known as Bloch states.²

Here n labels a band, k is crystal momentum, and uₙₖ carries the lattice-periodic structure of the wave function. Crystal momentum is defined modulo a reciprocal lattice vector, so its natural domain is the Brillouin zone. The important point is that a Bloch state is not a free-electron plane wave: it already includes the effect of the entire periodic arrangement of nuclei and other electrons in the chosen one-particle approximation.

Consequently, the perfectly static and periodic potential does not act as a collection of random scattering centers for its own Bloch eigenstates. An electron in such a state does not repeatedly lose crystal momentum by 'colliding with the nuclei.' The periodic lattice defines the states in which electronic motion takes place; it does not, by itself, produce dissipative scattering between those states.

This is the limited but useful sense in which a perfect crystal can appear vacuum-like to a Bloch electron. The analogy should not be taken literally. Its universe is far from empty: the lattice changes the relation between energy and momentum, introduces allowed and forbidden energy ranges, and gives Bloch wave packets velocities that need not resemble those of free particles. For an isolated band, the group velocity is vₙ(k) = (1/ℏ)∇ₖεₙ(k). Near a band extremum, the curvature of εₙ(k) determines an effective-mass tensor.

Bands explain the first difference between metals and insulators

Bloch's theorem tells us the form of the states; solving the crystal Hamiltonian determines their energies εₙ(k). For each momentum k there are many bands, and the electrons fill their available states subject to the Pauli principle. The occupancy of these states is just as important as their shape.

In an ordinary metal, there are partially occupied bands with states available close to the Fermi energy. In a conventional band insulator, the occupied bands are separated from the empty ones by an energy gap. A weak electric field cannot simply move an electron into an arbitrarily nearby available state if no such state exists. Thus an insulator need not contain fewer atomic electrons than a metal; it can fail to conduct because its low-energy electronic spectrum offers no mobile charge carriers.

A useful historical point is that the theory of bands was not only a method for drawing dispersion curves. It became a framework for predicting qualitative distinctions among materials. Wilson’s work illustrates how the availability of occupied and unoccupied states can matter more than any imagined frequency of collisions with individual nuclei.

This already explains why an account based only on collisions with nuclei is inadequate. Band structure and electron filling distinguish conductors from many insulators even before we calculate a scattering time. Disorder, localization, and strong electronic correlations can also produce insulating behavior, so a band gap is not the only possible mechanism. Still, the elementary band picture is the first major change of perspective: electronic transport is governed by quantum states, not by the geometric cross sections of nuclei.

02

What changes in a real crystal?

Imperfections and thermal motion

No physical crystal is perfectly periodic. Substitutional impurities, vacancies, interfaces, dislocations, and strain modify the local potential. At nonzero temperature, atoms fluctuate about their equilibrium positions, and electrons exchange energy and momentum with these lattice vibrations, or phonons. Electrons interact with one another as well. These processes must be added to the ideal band Hamiltonian to explain the finite resistivity of ordinary metals.

It is useful to separate two questions that a naive collision picture conflates. The first asks whether electronic states capable of carrying current are available. The second asks how a current-carrying state relaxes when impurities, phonons, or interactions are present. A typical metal has plenty of carriers but finite resistivity because their motion relaxes. A conventional band insulator has very few thermally excited carriers in the first place. Its large resistivity is not simply the consequence of more frequent collisions. In a metal, electron–electron scattering can redistribute momentum, but its ability to relax electric current depends on additional ingredients such as Umklapp processes or momentum-relaxing disorder.

Luttinger and Kohn made the next conceptual step: once the periodic crystal is solved, how does one describe an electron in a weakly perturbed crystal without repeating the full microscopic calculation? Their effective-mass theory gives a controlled answer near suitable band extrema. It also clarifies the limitation of the “vacuum and obstacles” analogy: an effective free-particle description is derived from Bloch states; it is not fundamental.

This overlap will become a recurring object in our discussion. It contains information about how the state changes with momentum. Its magnitude affects transition probabilities; its complex phase contains geometric information. Once wave functions enter even such a basic process as scattering, it becomes natural to ask what is missing when band theory is reduced to an energy-versus-momentum plot.

03

What an energy band leaves out

Energy eigenvalues are only half of the information

For an isolated, nondegenerate band, a Bloch Hamiltonian assigns each momentum both an eigenvalue εₙ(k) and an eigenstate |uₙ(k)⟩. The phase of the eigenstate is arbitrary, so a useful gauge-invariant description is its projector Pₙ(k) = |uₙ(k)⟩⟨uₙ(k)|. In general, we need both the energies and the projectors to specify the one-particle Hamiltonian:

Knowing εₙ(k) alone does not fix Pₙ(k). To see this, consider a two-level Hamiltonian H(k) = ε₀(k)I + Δ(k)n(k)·σ, with |n(k)| = 1. Its two energies are ε₀(k) ± Δ(k), regardless of which direction n(k) points. By changing the momentum-dependent direction of n(k), one can alter the eigenstates—and their geometric or topological structure—while keeping the energies unchanged. The example is mathematical but consequential: a dispersion plot cannot, by itself, tell us everything about a band.

The missing information is not the arbitrary overall phase chosen for each eigenvector. Physical quantities must be invariant under that choice. The nontrivial information lies instead in how the subspaces defined by the projectors change and fit together as k moves through the Brillouin zone.

There are two complementary ways to study this momentum dependence. Band topology asks whether a family of quantum states can be smoothly deformed into another while preserving appropriate gaps and symmetries. Quantum geometry describes how nearby states differ from one another. The distinction is similar to the difference between the global structure and local shape of a surface, although the relevant geometry is that of quantum states.

Historically, the geometric and topological developments were not a single linear sequence. The quantum-state metric was formulated in 1980, before the modern geometric-phase framework of 1984, while the 1982 analysis of quantized Hall transport established an especially direct link between band wave functions and topology. The following sections follow these strands separately before bringing them together.

04

Band topology: the global organization of states

The Brillouin zone is periodic in momentum space; in d spatial dimensions it has the topology of a d-dimensional torus. A band projector therefore defines a continuous map from a momentum-space torus into a space of quantum states. Two such maps may be topologically inequivalent even if their energy dispersions are identical. The inequivalence is expressed by topological invariants that remain unchanged under suitable smooth deformations.

A familiar example is a two-dimensional Chern band. The wave function defines a Berry connection Aᵢ(k) = i⟨u(k)|∂ᵢu(k)⟩, whose curl is the Berry curvature Ωₓᵧ(k). The Chern number is the integral

The Berry connection depends on the local choice of eigenstate phase, but its curvature and the integer C do not. This global invariant distinguishes bands that cannot be continuously connected without a relevant gap closing or a change in the assumptions of the classification. In suitable settings, it also constrains robust boundary transport. Not every topological invariant, however, leads to identical boundary behavior.

The Open Lab presentation also drew attention to the bulk–boundary connection. Callan and Harvey offered a field-theoretic interpretation of how chiral boundary or domain-wall modes can compensate the anomaly of a bulk topological term. That is a powerful idea for appropriate gapped phases, but it must not be taken to imply that every kind of delicate band topology guarantees an ordinary metallic surface.

The significance of the TKNN formula is not that every band with nonzero Berry curvature is an insulator with a quantized Hall response: quantization requires the appropriate filling and a spectral or mobility gap. Berry’s later geometric-phase framework made the underlying connection more general, and Haldane supplied a particularly illuminating lattice model. Zak then showed how a one-dimensional Brillouin-zone cycle can itself carry a geometric phase.

A Chern band without Landau levels

A two-dimensional lattice model can exhibit a quantized Hall response even when the net magnetic flux per unit cell vanishes.

Why it matters The topological response can arise from Bloch-band structure itself, not only from continuum Landau levels.

F. D. M. Haldane, Phys. Rev. Lett. 61, 2015 (1988). ↗

The real Hopf insulator is an example where the full multiband wave-function structure matters. In a particular four-band system with spinless PT symmetry, both the two occupied and the two unoccupied states enter the relevant topology. With the necessary orientability and other conditions imposed, the state space is related to the product S² × S², and its third homotopy group permits two independent Hopf-type invariants. This is a delicate form of topology: it depends on the chosen multiband structure and can be lost when that structure is enlarged or otherwise modified.

There is a useful analogy with gauge flux. Bloch eigenstates determine a Berry connection in momentum space, analogous to a gauge potential, and Berry curvature plays the role of geometric field strength. A noncontractible cycle of the Brillouin-zone torus can carry nontrivial Berry holonomy, much as a gauge flux threading the handle of a ring produces an Aharonov–Bohm phase. For a two-dimensional momentum-space cycle, the integral of Berry curvature gives a geometric flux whose quantization defines a Chern number. These are related but distinct notions: holonomy can be nontrivial even when the local curvature vanishes. The loop winding drawn below is a visual analogy, not the definition of the real Hopf invariant. Real Hopf topology instead involves a three-dimensional momentum-space map and the linking of preimages in its state space; it need not carry nonzero net first-Chern flux.

This example shows why describing only the filled states may be insufficient for certain topological questions. It also illustrates why one should distinguish topological surface phenomena tied to a specific multiband structure from the universal gapless surface spectrum associated with some stable topological insulators. Topology answers questions about the global organization of states, but that is not the end of the story.

The Hopf example is historically a later branch of this development, not the original entry point to topological band theory. The conventional quantum Hall and Chern-insulator results explain why global band structure matters in the first place; real Hopf topology then demonstrates how the classification can depend sensitively on the full multiband embedding.

05

Quantum geometry: how quickly states change

There is a subtle chronological point here: the quantum metric was introduced by Provost and Vallée in 1980, and the phase structure was developed by Berry in 1984. In the language of the quantum geometric tensor, these two perspectives become parts of the same mathematical object. Neither is merely a decorative analogy with the geometry of ordinary surfaces.

These objects have measurable consequences. Berry curvature enters the anomalous velocity of semiclassical wave packets and contributes to transverse electronic responses. The quantum metric is connected to wave-function localization, optical matrix elements, and other response properties. In two dimensions, positivity of the quantum geometric tensor implies an inequality such as tr g ≥ |Ωₓᵧ|, in orthonormal momentum coordinates. This places a local geometric constraint on the possible relation between metric and curvature.

The Wannier formulation gave quantum geometry a particularly tangible meaning. Asking how localized an electronic orbital can be in real space becomes, in part, a question about how the corresponding Bloch subspace varies over momentum space. This is a useful example of a geometric quantity entering an experimentally relevant property without requiring a topologically nontrivial band.

A key distinction now emerges. Integrating Berry curvature over the Brillouin zone can yield an integer topological invariant, but the distribution of curvature and the metric across the zone are not fixed by that integer. Two bands can share the same Chern number and yet display very different local quantum geometry. Thus the topology of a band does not exhaust the information carried by its wave functions.

The Open Lab presentation asks a natural next question: can the geometry be measured? A 2020 theory of anomalous Landau levels connected quantum distance with magnetic spectra in singular flat bands. More recently, ARPES-based experiments have accessed quantum geometry directly in particular solids. These developments should be kept distinct: an indirect theoretical proposal, a spectroscopy-based reconstruction, and a direct metric measurement are not the same achievement. The two 2025 results concern particular settings rather than the fully general multiband, band-pair-resolved QGT.

06

From Chern bands to Kramers pairs

The relation between quantum metric and Berry curvature becomes especially interesting in the study of narrow bands and correlated phases. Certain ideal Chern bands possess a special compatibility between metric and curvature, naturally described by Kähler geometry. This makes their single-particle states resemble, in important ways, those of a Landau level. Such relations offer valuable geometric criteria for identifying promising settings for fractional Chern insulators. They do not, on their own, guarantee that interactions will stabilize a particular many-body phase.

The historical development from geometry to correlated-matter questions is also more recent than the original quantum metric. Roy identified ideal band-geometric conditions under which a projected density algebra becomes Landau-level-like; Peotta and Törmä later exhibited an explicit role for the quantum metric in flat-band superfluid weight. These are different results, but both show why geometry cannot simply be discarded when band kinetic energy is small.

Time-reversal-symmetric systems introduce additional structure. When spinful bands form a Kramers-degenerate pair—for example, in a system with both inversion and time reversal symmetry—the occupied states at a given k span a two-dimensional subspace rather than a single complex line. Geometric quantities associated with that subspace are matrix-valued, and the Berry connection and curvature become non-Abelian. In the quaternionic setting, the corresponding SU(2) curvature has three internal components that are related to the metric.

My work on quaternion-Kähler geometry examines this extension of the geometric language of ideal Chern bands to Kramers pairs. In the simplest quaternionic models, the geometry can be organized using quaternionic projective spaces and local metric–curvature relations. This does not establish an interacting topological phase in a time-reversal-symmetric material. It supplies a framework in which to formulate the geometric conditions that such a search may require.

07

When electrons become one another's environment

Up to this point, we have largely discussed the states of a single electron in a background crystal. A material is, of course, a many-electron system. Other electrons are not merely a static part of the landscape: their motion responds to, and modifies, the motion of the electron we are following. The question becomes how interactions reorganize the many-electron states that band theory makes available.

When the dispersion is narrow, the latter can become particularly important. This is a concrete reason that single-particle quantum geometry may influence collective states—even though a calculation of the interacting Hamiltonian is still required to determine what actually emerges.

We can now return to the initial question about resistance and the striking diversity of materials. The basic ingredients of matter may be similar, but their electrons do not inhabit the same quantum-mechanical environment. Atomic order creates Bloch states and bands; filling and gaps govern the availability of carriers; imperfections and fluctuations govern relaxation; and wave-function topology and geometry constrain processes that energy dispersions alone cannot describe. Interactions add a further level, where new collective forms of matter can emerge.

That is what I mean by asking how an electron sees its universe. The question is a way of moving from a picture of particles colliding with atoms to a theory of the quantum states that make a material what it is.
